\subsection{Grid city}

Infinite grid of integer locations $(x,y)$, with $m,n\ge0$:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $s_{\text{start}}=(0,0)$, \quad $\text{IsEnd}(s)=\indic{s=(m,n)}$
  \item $\text{Actions}(s)=\{(+1,0),(-1,0),(0,+1),(0,-1)\}$, \quad $\text{Succ}(s,a)=s+a$
  \item $\text{Cost}((x,y),a)=1+\max(x,0)$
\end{itemize}

\problempart{1a}{Minimum cost}
\question{What is the minimum cost of reaching $(m,n)$ from $(0,0)$? Describe a path achieving it.
Is it unique?}
\expect{An expression for the minimum cost; 1--2 sentences describing one optimal path and whether it is unique.}
\begin{solution}
Minimum cost
\[
\boxed{\;n+\sum_{x=0}^{m-1}(1+x)=n+m+\frac{m(m-1)}{2}=n+\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\;}.
\]
Every path must contain at least $m$ east moves and $n$ north moves. A north (or any) move costs $1$ when
taken at $x\le0$ and $1+x\ge2$ when taken at $x\ge1$, so all north moves should be made before leaving $x=0$;
the $k$-th east move necessarily starts at $x=k-1$ and costs $1+(k-1)$. Detours (west, south, or extra
east/west pairs) only add moves of cost $\ge1$, so they never help. One optimal path is therefore: $n$ steps
north from $(0,0)$ to $(0,n)$, then $m$ steps east to $(m,n)$. It is unique whenever $m\ge1$ and $n\ge1$: any
north move taken at $x\ge1$ costs at least $2$ instead of $1$, and moving to $x<0$ first costs two extra
unit moves, so no other path attains the minimum. (If $m=0$ or $n=0$ the straight line is trivially the only
optimum.)
\end{solution}

\problempart{1b}{UCS on this problem}
\question{True or false:
(i) UCS never terminates because there are infinitely many states.
(ii) UCS returns the minimum-cost path and explores only $(x,y)$ with $0\le x\le m$, $0\le y\le n$.
(iii) UCS returns the minimum-cost path and explores only locations whose past cost is $\le$ the minimum cost to $(m,n)$.}
\expect{T/F for each sub-part.}
\begin{solution}
\begin{description}[nosep]
  \item[(i) False.] All costs are $\ge1$, so only finitely many locations have past cost at most the (finite)
  optimal cost $C^\star$. UCS pops states in increasing order of past cost, so it reaches the goal and stops
  after exploring finitely many states; an infinite state space is not a problem as long as positive costs
  bound the number of states cheaper than the goal.
  \item[(ii) False.] UCS explores every state whose past cost is below $C^\star$, and many of them lie outside
  the rectangle: $(-1,0)$ has past cost $1$, $(0,n+1)$ has past cost $n+1\le C^\star$ (for $m\ge1$), and all
  of $(-k,y)$ with $k+y<C^\star$ are explored too. UCS does return the minimum-cost path, but the second half
  of the statement is wrong.
  \item[(iii) True.] UCS pops states from the frontier in non-decreasing order of past cost and terminates when
  it pops the goal (at past cost $C^\star$); hence every explored state has past cost $\le C^\star$, and the
  returned path is optimal because all costs are non-negative.
\end{description}
\end{solution}

\problempart{1c}{UCS on an arbitrary graph}
\question{True or false:
(i) Adding a connection between two nodes cannot increase the minimum cost between any start and end node.
(ii) Making an action's cost small enough (possibly negative) guarantees that it appears in the path UCS returns, if a path exists.
(iii) Increasing every action's cost by 1 does not change the minimum-cost path, only its cost.}
\expect{T/F for each sub-part.}
\begin{solution}
\begin{description}[nosep]
  \item[(i) True.] Adding a connection only adds new paths; every path that existed before still exists with
  the same cost, so the minimum over the (larger) set of paths cannot increase.
  \item[(ii) False.] Two reasons. The cheap action may not lie on \emph{any} start--end path (e.g.\ an edge
  into a dead end, or one pointing away from the goal that can only be followed by expensive moves back), so no
  path containing it is ever returned. And if the cost is made negative, UCS is no longer correct at all: it
  assumes non-negative costs, so it may return a path without the cheap edge even when a cheaper path using it
  exists (or, with a negative cycle, there is no finite minimum).
  \item[(iii) False.] Adding $1$ to every action penalises paths with more actions. Example: path $P_1$ has three
  edges of cost $1$ (total $3$) and path $P_2$ one edge of cost $4$. Originally $P_1$ is optimal ($3<4$); after
  the change $P_1$ costs $6$ and $P_2$ costs $5$, so the minimum-cost path switches to $P_2$.
\end{description}
\end{solution}

\subsection{Finding shortest paths}

\problempart{2a}{\texttt{ShortestPathProblem}}
\codepart{3.2a}

\problempart{2b}{Stanford shortest-path experiments}
\question{Choose start locations and end tags with \texttt{get\_stanford\_shortest\_path\_problem}, and
visualise them. Give (i) a setting that gave new insight into travelling around campus (was the system useful?) and
(ii) a setting where the minimum-cost path is \emph{not} desirable. Is that caused by modelling assumptions
(missing tags or paths, inaccurate locations or distances)?}
\expect{Screenshots of both visualisations; one or more sentences on something learned about travelling, and
something incorrect about the map or the modelling assumptions.}
\begin{solution}
Both routes were produced by \texttt{ShortestPathProblem} + UCS on the Stanford map and drawn with the
provided \texttt{visualization.py} (exported to PNG by \texttt{scripts/route\_figures.py}). Red marker = start,
green marker = end; blue dots are \texttt{amenity} locations and purple dots landmarks.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
  \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{03_route/2b_insight.png}
    \caption{\texttt{landmark=gates} $\to$ \texttt{amenity=food}: 659\,m, 38 locations; ends at the Cantor Arts
    Center caf\'e.}
  \end{subfigure}\hfill
  \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{03_route/2b_undesirable.png}
    \caption{\texttt{landmark=hoover\_tower} $\to$ \texttt{amenity=food}: 447\,m walked to a caf\'e that is only
    137\,m away in a straight line.}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{Problem 2b: shortest paths on the Stanford map.}
\end{figure}
\textbf{(i) New insight.} Our custom problem (\texttt{get\_stanford\_shortest\_path\_problem}) asks for the
nearest place with food when leaving the Gates building. We expected Coupa Caf\'e at Green Library or
Tresidder, but the search returns the caf\'e at the Cantor Arts Center, 659\,m away, which is closer than either
(Tresidder is $>$700\,m). The system is useful precisely because the end is given as a \emph{tag} rather than a
fixed destination: it searched all four food locations at once and found an option we would not have thought of.

\textbf{(ii) Undesirable path.} From Hoover Tower the nearest food is Coupa Caf\'e at Green Library, 137\,m away
as the crow flies, yet the minimum-cost path is 447\,m long (3.3$\times$ the straight-line distance) and loops
around the surrounding buildings. This is a modelling problem, not a search problem: the map only contains the
footpaths present in the OpenStreetMap extract (and filters out ways tagged as not walkable), so the direct
paths across the Hoover courtyard and through the library arcade are simply missing, and every landmark is
snapped to the nearest OSM node within 250\,m, which can put it on the ``wrong'' side of a building. A
second, smaller issue is the tags themselves: Stanford Stadium is tagged \texttt{amenity=food} although its
concessions only operate on game days, so a hungry person at the stadium would be told they have already
arrived (a 0\,m path), and no tag carries opening hours or whether a place is indoors.
\end{solution}

\problempart{2c}{Negative externalities}
\question{What negative externalities could follow from wide deployment of this shortest-path system? Discuss
the effects on (1) users and (2) non-users, and ways to reduce the mismatch between the model and the real world
(see ``Why Traffic Apps Make Congestion Worse'', ``The Perfect Selfishness of Mapping Apps'', and the
Externalities module).}
\expect{3--5 sentences: two externalities (one for users, one for non-users) and at least one mitigation.}
\begin{solution}
Our model treats each traveller in isolation on a static map, but in reality every routed person changes the
conditions for everyone else. \emph{For users:} if a large fraction of people receive the same ``shortest''
route, that route becomes congested, so the predicted travel time is wrong and users end up slower than the
system promised (the ``selfish routing'' effect described in the articles, where individually optimal
choices produce a collectively worse equilibrium). \emph{For non-users:} the shortest routes often cut through
quiet residential streets, narrow campus paths or bike lanes, so residents, pedestrians and cyclists who never
asked for the system absorb more traffic, noise, pollution and accident risk, while the costs of their roads
being used are not counted anywhere in our objective. The root cause is the mismatch between the model
(fixed edge costs, independent travellers) and the world (costs that depend on load and on externalities).
Ways to reduce it: make edge costs depend on predicted load and update them from live data; add the social
cost of a path (residential streets, school zones, emissions) to the cost function instead of only distance;
and deliberately spread users over several near-optimal routes rather than sending everyone down the same
one.
\end{solution}

\subsection{Finding shortest paths with unordered waypoints}

\problempart{3a}{\texttt{WaypointsShortestPathProblem}}
\codepart{3.3a}

\problempart{3b}{Size of the state space}
\question{With $n$ locations and $k$ waypoint tags, what is the maximum number of states UCS could visit
under a suitable state definition for 3a?}
\expect{An expression in $n$ and $k$ with a brief justification.}
\begin{solution}
At most $\boxed{n\cdot 2^{k}}$ states. Our state is (current location, set of waypoint tags covered so far).
The location takes one of $n$ values and the covered set is one of the $2^k$ subsets of the $k$ waypoint
tags, so there are $n\cdot2^k$ distinct states and UCS can visit each at most once. Nothing else about the
past (the exact locations visited, their order, the distance travelled) affects which future paths are
feasible or their costs, so this is the smallest sufficient memory; in practice far fewer states are reached,
because tags are only picked up at the few locations that carry them.
\end{solution}

\problempart{3c}{Stanford waypoints experiment}
\question{Choose a start location, waypoint tags (at least two) and an end tag with
\texttt{get\_stanford\_waypoints\_shortest\_path\_problem}, and visualise the route. What is unexpected or
interesting about it? Does it match your expectations, and what important factors does it fail to capture?}
\expect{A screenshot, the list of waypoint tags, and one or more sentences of discussion.}
\begin{solution}
Our custom problem (\texttt{get\_stanford\_waypoints\_shortest\_path\_problem}) is an end-of-day errand run:
start at the Rains graduate housing, cover the waypoint tags
\texttt{amenity=food}, \texttt{landmark=bookstore} and \texttt{landmark=green\_library} in any order, and end
at \texttt{landmark=gates}.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{03_route/3c_waypoints.png}
  \caption{Problem 3c: Rains $\to$ \{food, bookstore, Green Library\} $\to$ Gates. Total length 2\,256\,m over
  160 locations; UCS explored 22\,557 states (vs.\ $\approx$2\,000 for the plain shortest-path queries above).}
\end{figure}
The solver chose the order Rains $\to$ Bookstore $\to$ Green Library $\to$ Gates, 2\,256\,m in total, and it
satisfied the food requirement ``for free'': Coupa Caf\'e sits at the same map node as Green Library, so one
location covers two tags and no detour to Tresidder or the Cantor caf\'e is needed. That is exactly the kind of
shortcut the unordered-waypoint formulation is meant to discover, and it matches what a person who knows the
campus would do. Two things the path fails to capture: it treats the Green Library node as ``food'' even though
Coupa's opening hours or a long queue might make Tresidder the better stop, and it minimises distance only,
ignoring that the Bookstore closes early, so a feasible plan at 20:00 would have to be a different, longer
tour. The state space also grew by a factor of about $2^k=8$, as predicted in 3b.
\end{solution}

\problempart{3d}{Ride-sharing and dual use}
\question{Ride-sharing apps use similar routing and have been criticised for exploitative nudges (e.g.\
``forward dispatch''). How could unordered waypoints improve the driver experience between a drop-off and the
next pick-up, and what driver information would you need? As a dual-use concern, how could a company use the
feature to raise profits at the expense of driver health, or what are the downsides of collecting that information?}
\expect{3--5 sentences: (a) waypoints that improve the driver experience; (b) at least one dimension of
\emph{driver identity} (not trip attributes such as time of day) that informs the choice; (c) a harmful use or a
downside of the data collection.}
\begin{solution}
\textbf{(a) Waypoints that help drivers.} Between a drop-off and the next pick-up the dispatcher could route
the driver through waypoints such as a rest area or caf\'e with parking (\texttt{amenity=cafe},
\texttt{amenity=toilets}), a fuel or charging station when the tank or battery is low, a place of worship at
prayer times, or simply a quiet spot to take a legally required break; because the waypoints are unordered,
the driver still gets the shortest tour that covers them before arriving at the next rider.
\textbf{(b) Driver information needed.} Choosing appropriate waypoints requires information about the driver
as a person, not about the trip: e.g.\ religion (prayer times and places), dietary needs (halal, vegetarian
food stops), disability or health conditions (accessible toilets, medication breaks), and preferences about
when and how often they rest. \textbf{(c) Dual use.} The same feature lets a company maximise profit at the
driver's expense: it can insert waypoints that are good for the business (surge-pricing zones, partner petrol
stations, areas where the next pick-up is likely) and keep the driver in constant motion, turning the
``break'' feature into forward dispatch under another name; and collecting religion, health and routine data
creates a sensitive profile that can leak, be sold, or be used to discriminate (e.g.\ assigning fewer rides to
drivers who take breaks), so drivers may reasonably refuse to share it.
\end{solution}
